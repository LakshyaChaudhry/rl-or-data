# Reviewer B: Novelty and venue fit

**Manuscript reviewed:** *Is it RL or Data? A Controlled Comparison of GRPO and Rejection-Sampling Fine-Tuning* (AAAI-27 kit PDF, 6 pages). Canonical preprint related work and conclusion were read as arXiv:2610.25812v1 source. No venue is named in the manuscript. NeSy-FM at AAAI-27 is assessed only as a tentative candidate.

**Recommendation:** The body already scopes the work to one counting task, one base model, LoRA, and three seeds, and it declines to contradict Xiong et al. (2025) and Samineni et al. (2025). Under the current title, the distinct contribution does not survive the manuscript's own account of Bauer et al. (2026), Lu et al. (2026), Xiong et al. (2025), Samineni et al. (2025), Zhu et al. (2025), and Yue et al. (2025). I would not accept the paper under this title. I would also treat a NeSy-FM submission as off-topic.

## Assigned checks

These three checks hold. They are not suggestions to weaken the draft.

**Failed H2 stays visible.** The abstract (p. 1) says curating prompts does not help RFT, contrary to the registered prediction. Section 1 repeats that the registered prediction fails. Section 2.3 (p. 2) states H2 in its registered form: curation would recover most of GRPO's advantage over RFT-Mixed. Section 3.2 (p. 4) reports the outcome: RFT-Curated is 2.7 points below RFT-Mixed on test, interval \([-5.2, 0.0]\), all three seed pairs negative, OOD sign flipped, registered rule failed, and the share of the 8.2-point GRPO-Curated minus RFT-Mixed gap recovered by curation is negative. Section 6 (p. 5) repeats that curation, predicted to recover most of the gap for RFT, does not help RFT. The preprint conclusion says the same thing at the same strength.

**The paper does not claim to contradict Xiong et al. or Samineni et al.** The abstract and Section 1 (p. 1) cite them for the finding that filtered fine-tuning on samples from the current policy can perform close to GRPO, and they describe this experiment as a weaker data condition: one draw from the base model, plus a secondary arm that refreshes the sampler three times. Section 3.4 (p. 4) says the three-round arm does not contradict studies whose filtered methods resample every iteration. Section 4 (p. 5) says the 8–11 point test gap is a different setting (at most 100 prompts, one base-model draw, several epochs on the kept set) and "We do not read it as evidence against those studies." Section 5 (p. 5) limits the result to GRPO outperforming this RFT recipe at this budget, and withholds a claim about filtered fine-tuning in general. The preprint related work is explicit: "our results do not contradict theirs. What they show is narrower: data sampled once from the base model was not enough in our setting." The condensation keeps that stance.

**Scope stays on one counting task, one Qwen3-4B-Base model, LoRA, and three seeds.** The abstract's last sentence (p. 1), the first sentence of Section 5 (p. 5), and the last sentence of Section 6 (p. 5) all say so. Section 4 (p. 5) adds that Bauer et al. studied counting, graph, and spatial tasks and that this paper uses counting only. The preprint conclusion matches. The title itself carries no scope line. That belongs with the title finding below, and it does not erase the scope sentences in the body.

## Blocker

**B1. Once the manuscript's own descriptions of the six papers are applied, the titled contribution is gone.**

The title on p. 1 asks "Is it RL or Data?" Section 6 answers with a refusal: "None of these statements is a general claim that reinforcement learning is more than the data." Section 5 says the design does not separate negative samples, group-relative advantages, and the number of policy refreshes. A title that poses a separation the limitations section says the design leaves bundled is the contribution claim a reader, a chair, and a citation will keep. The body already argues for a smaller paper than the title submits.

What the manuscript itself says about each paper, and what that leaves:

- **Bauer et al. (2026).** Section 1 (p. 1) uses them as the reason for the paper: in a low-data regime, mixed-difficulty prompts made GRPO much more sample-efficient than easy-only prompts on a procedural counting task, and they did not test whether that benefit is specific to GRPO. Section 4 (p. 5) says they are the source of the task, the model family, and the GRPO recipe, and that this paper uses counting only. Section 2.3 (p. 2) then records two departures from that recipe: binary correctness without their format bonus and step-count penalty, and 8 prompts per step. Section 2.1 adds two generator constraints (every step changes the set; stratification by range size and step count) and measures difficulty on the model being trained. The manuscript never measures whether those generator changes matter. Section 3.3 (p. 4) is the within-method test of the benefit Section 1 says Bauer et al. left unassigned: mixed versus easy for GRPO, and the same contrast for RFT. The GRPO half fails the paper's own rule: GRPO-Mixed minus GRPO-Easy is \(+4.4\) on test, below the \(2\sigma\) threshold of 5.6, the OOD sign reverses, and every GRPO-Mixed seed is truncation-flagged. The sentence in Section 3.3 is "For GRPO itself, this experiment does not confirm it." The RFT half is the \(+3.7\) point mixed-versus-easy gap, which Section 3.3 and Section 6 place next to an epoch confound (8 versus 4) and cross-machine variation of up to 2.3 points on test. Relative to Bauer, the open question is unanswered for GRPO, under a recipe the paper says it changed, on one of their three tasks.

- **Lu et al. (2026).** Section 1 (p. 1) says they attribute part of RL's advantage over supervised fine-tuning to an implicit difficulty filter, and draws the conditional: if the filter is the whole story, a supervised method given the same prompts should recover the gain. Section 4 (p. 5) narrows what was actually compared: Lu et al. show that a difficulty filter can help supervised fine-tuning in vision-language settings; the curated set matches their medium-difficulty definition on eight samples from the initial model; in this setting that filter did not help RFT; RFT trains on the model's own reasoning rather than on ground-truth labels. The preprint related work goes further and should not have been cut: their strongest variant also keeps the easy samples, "which we did not test as a separate arm," and RFT's sample counts still follow the base model's pass rate. The workshop draft still says the settings differ, and Section 2.2 (p. 2) records the pass-rate dominance directly: 595 of 7,961 kept RFT-Mixed samples come from hard prompts. H2 is a real failed prediction about this curation on this RFT recipe. It is visible, and it is the only result that could have been a distinct empirical statement. It is not a distinct statement about Lu et al., because the manuscript's own related work places Lu et al. in another modality, another training target, and, in the preprint, an untested stronger variant.

- **Xiong et al. (2025) and Samineni et al. (2025).** Section 4 (p. 5), using only the workshop wording: Xiong et al. find that keeping only positively rewarded samples is competitive with GRPO, on a much larger prompt pool. Samineni et al. argue that, under standard assumptions, GRPO reduces to an iterative filtered supervised update, and they report comparable performance, including with negative samples. The workshop experiment sits on the other side of those stated conditions: positive samples only, one draw from the base model, at most 100 prompts, and either no refresh or three refreshes against GRPO's 300 (Section 2.2). Section 4 calls the resulting 8–11 point gap a different setting. A loss by that weaker RFT recipe does not separate this paper from results the manuscript attributes to a much larger pool, an iterative filtered update, and, for Samineni et al., negative samples. The manuscript agrees. Section 3.4 uses the three-round arm as the move toward their regime and reports that it recovers 3.2 of the 10.9 points, leaves 7.7, and still does not contradict them. The preprint's "what they show is narrower" sentence is the right contribution statement. The title asks a broader question.

- **Zhu et al. (2025).** Section 4 says reinforcing correct samples raises pass@1 and can lower pass@\(k\), while penalizing incorrect samples often matches GRPO. Section 3.1 (p. 3) reports agreement on the coverage pattern and says "It is not a new coverage result." Section 3.4's residual 70% explicitly includes "learning from failed samples," and Section 5 says that term is not separated from group-relative advantages and the number of refreshes. Zhu et al.'s matching condition, penalizing incorrect samples, is one of the unseparated terms. The paper does not run their split.

- **Yue et al. (2025).** They appear in Section 3.1 (p. 3) and the reference list. The manuscript says the pass@\(k\) pattern agrees with them and is not a new coverage result. On the first 100 test problems, pass@64 is 98.0% for the base model and 95.7–96.7% for the GRPO arms. That ceiling leaves almost no room in which a capacity increase could have appeared. Agreement at that ceiling is a confirmation with little resolving power, which Section 3.1 already grants.

The distinct remainder is a scoped measurement: on this counting task, this inherited-and-modified GRPO recipe beats this one-shot RFT recipe by about 8–11 test points, explicit curation does not hand that gap to RFT, and three RFT rounds recover a minority of it. That is a workshop note the body already writes in Section 5 and Section 6. It is not a new account of whether reinforcement learning is more than the data, and it is not a result those six papers lack once their claims are stated as this manuscript states them.

## Major

**M1. The abstract and Section 1 still headline a pre-registered success that the protocol, on the same pages, disqualifies.**

The abstract (p. 1) leads with GRPO ahead of RFT by 10.9 points on the 300-problem test set, then says that comparison was pre-registered. Section 1 (p. 1) says the registered comparison "meets the paper's success criterion on test and has the same sign out of distribution, with the truncation caveat above."

Three passages in the same document qualify that sentence.

- Section 2.3 (p. 2) defines the rule as an absolute test gap larger than twice the pooled seed standard deviation, plus the same sign on `ood_hard_200`, and says the rule is not a formal test. The next sentence says runs with more than 5% truncation on `test_300` are flagged and are not headline numbers as they stand. Section 3.1 (p. 3) reports that every GRPO-Curated seed exceeds the flag (5.3%, 9.3%, 8.0%). Table 2 (p. 3) marks GRPO-Curated and GRPO-Mixed with that flag. The abstract does disclose the flag and the adverse bound. It still leads with the flagged 10.9.
- Section 2.3 (p. 2) says the experiment as run does not match the registered H3 sentence on sampling budgets. H3 required identical prompt and sampling budgets. The curated pair matches prompts and mismatches rollouts: 19,200 versus 14,016. The abstract calls the 10.9-point comparison pre-registered and does not mention that budget clause. Section 6 (p. 5) again calls it "the curated test comparison that was registered in advance." The arms were registered. The sampling budget named in H3 is the budget Section 2.3 says was not run. The post hoc easy and mixed contrasts, which do match rollout counts, are the ones the abstract correctly labels post hoc. The registered cell is the unmatched one.
- The OOD piece of the success rule is a sign. Table 3 (p. 4) gives the curated OOD gap as \(+3.0\) with interval \([-2.5, +8.5]\). Section 3.1 says the sign check passes and the OOD estimate is still weak, and that 20–55% of GRPO completions are cut on OOD, so the paper draws no OOD conclusion. Section 5 repeats the refusal. "Meets the success criterion" and "has the same sign out of distribution" in Section 1 are a sign check on a difference the limitations section refuses to interpret. The abstract's own sentence is better: out of distribution, truncation is too common for a conclusion. Section 1 walks back toward a success claim.

The adverse bound (every truncated RFT completion counted correct, none of GRPO's) is reported in Section 3.1 as \(+7.8\), \(+6.0\), and \(+7.2\) points on curated, easy, and mixed. The abstract's "still leaves a gap" matches those signs. Section 2.3 lists the truncation bounds among the analyses added after the primary test results were seen. The abstract does not label that bound post hoc. The bound is a sensitivity check on a flagged, budget-unmatched contrast. It can support "the test ordering stays positive under this adverse scoring." It cannot promote the 10.9 into a clean pre-registered headline.

**M2. "Controlled," "one inherited recipe," and "harder to explain as a weak baseline" describe a comparison Section 2.3 and Section 5 say is unmatched on the axes that carry the mechanism.**

The subtitle calls the study a controlled comparison. Section 2.3 (p. 2) states the unmatched pieces: curated rollouts differ; training tokens and optimizer steps are not equalized; RFT trains for several epochs on correct samples only; GRPO trains once on every sample and uses within-group comparisons. Table 1 (p. 3) makes the scale concrete: RFT-Curated uses 6,653 of 14,016 rollouts, 13.4M tokens, and 1,664 optimizer steps; GRPO-Curated uses 19,200 of 19,200 rollouts, 20.6M tokens, and 300 steps. Selected RFT hyperparameters also differ by arm (Easy \(5\times10^{-5}\) for 8 epochs, Mixed \(5\times10^{-5}\) for 4 epochs, Curated \(10^{-5}\) for 4 epochs), which Section 5 says confounds the RFT data comparisons.

The abstract says GRPO uses one inherited recipe. Section 2.3 lists the two departures above (reward, and prompts per step). Those departures sit on the GRPO side of the Bauer comparison in B1. "Inherited" without the departures is how the abstract and Section 4 ("the GRPO recipe we inherit") will be quoted.

Section 1 says RFT is tuned so that a GRPO lead is harder to explain as a weak baseline. The sweep is a \(3\times3\) over learning rate and epochs, 27 runs, selected on 100 validation problems (Section 2.3). Section 5 locates the baseline weakness elsewhere: no per-prompt cap, so easy prompts dominate the kept set; one sample from the base model; the iterated arm refreshes only three times; single-round RFT does not improve greedy test accuracy over the base model (67.3%), so the GRPO gap is about as large as GRPO's gain over the base model. The sweep does not touch that weakness. A lead over a one-shot, easy-dominated, positive-only filter is the lead Section 5 describes. Calling the 27-run sweep the answer to "weak baseline" aims the reader's skepticism at the learning rate and leaves the data condition, which is the actual contact with Xiong et al. and Samineni et al., outside the claim.

## Minor

**m1. Two abstract sentences are stronger than the sections that own the numbers.**

"Mixed-difficulty data helps RFT by 3.7 points" (p. 1). Table 3 (p. 4) shows \(+3.7\) with interval \([+0.2, +7.0]\) and a "yes" under the paper's rule. Section 3.3 (p. 4) adds the epoch confound, the shared RFT draw (the \(2\sigma\) threshold omits sampling variance), and the comparison with 2.3 points of cross-machine test variation. Section 6 calls it a small margin that sits near that variation. "Helps" in the abstract is a causal verb the conclusion will not use.

"Three rounds of iterated RFT, a secondary arm, recover 3.2 of the 10.9-point gap" (p. 1). The 3.2 and the arithmetic \(3.2+7.7=10.9\) are in Section 3.4 (p. 4). The same subsection says the 30% figure is a description, not a registered fraction, and is not a pure effect of fresher samples: iterated RFT also takes more optimizer steps (1,963 versus 1,664) and more kept samples (7,828 versus 6,653), and the 3.2-point gain is close to the 2.3-point cross-machine figure. "Recover" assigns the 3.2 to policy refresh. Section 3.4 withholds that assignment.

**m2. Section 1's filter conditional is broader than the RFT test, and the condensation drops a preprint sentence that kept Lu et al. at a distance.**

Section 1 (p. 1): if the filter is the whole story, a supervised method given the same prompts should recover the gain. The method that was run keeps correct traces from one base-model draw, with counts that follow the pass rate (Section 2.2). Section 4 says Lu et al. concern vision-language supervised fine-tuning on ground-truth labels. Those are the manuscript's words, and they already separate the conditional from the experiment. The preprint related work also says Lu et al.'s strongest variant keeps the easy samples and that this paper did not test that arm. Section 4 of the workshop draft omits that sentence, so "in our setting that filter did not help RFT" reads closer to a result about their filter than the preprint allows. Restoring the preprint sentence would match the non-contradiction care already used for Xiong et al. and Samineni et al.

## Nit

**n1. The Yue et al. agreement is reported where the base model has almost no uncovered mass.** Pass@64 on the first 100 test problems is 98.0% for the base model (Section 3.1, p. 3). Citing agreement with Yue et al. (2025) and the pass@\(k\) split in Zhu et al. (2025) is consistent with the text. It cannot do much work, and Yue et al. do not appear in Section 4 at all.

**n2. GSM8K numbers sit in Section 3.4 (p. 4) with no formal comparison.** Base 88.0%, single-round RFT 90.9–91.9%, GRPO 92.7–93.3%, iterated RFT 92.1%, truncation at most 1.0%. The paragraph says no comparisons were computed. The ordering is still on the page, on a transfer set Section 2.1 called secondary. The scope checks above survive because the paper withholds a comparison. The numbers will be read as one.

**n3. Table 2's Qwen3-4B reference row is never interpreted.** It scores 74.7 on test and 45.5 on OOD, above every trained arm on OOD. The caption says it is a single run. No sentence in Sections 3–6 uses it. It is not a second trained model, and it should not be asked to extend the scope. An unused row in the main results table invites that reading anyway.

## Closing answers

**Is the scientific claim in the abstract defensible?** As a scoped report of this run, mostly yes. The 10.9, the 5% flag on every GRPO seed in that cell, the adverse bound, the refusal of an OOD conclusion, the post hoc 8.4 and 9.2, the failed curation prediction, the 3.7, the 3.2, and the closing scope sentence (one synthetic task family, one base model, LoRA, three seeds) all have matching passages in Tables 2–3 and Sections 3.1–3.4. I do not find an invented result in the abstract. It is not defensible as an answer to the title, and the abstract does not quite stay inside the protocol that surrounds its lead number. "That comparison was pre-registered" omits the H3 sampling-budget deviation in Section 2.3 (19,200 versus 14,016 rollouts). The 10.9 is a truncation-flagged figure that Section 2.3 says is not a headline number as it stands. "Helps" for the 3.7 and "recover" for the 3.2 are stronger than Sections 3.3, 3.4, and 6. Read with its last sentence governing the first, the abstract can stand as a description of one GRPO recipe against one RFT recipe. Read as evidence that the gap is the training signal, it is ahead of Section 5, which still leaves rollout count, truncation, and the one-shot filter unresolved.

**Would a NeSy-FM reviewer have grounds to call it off-topic?** Yes. The author has not selected a venue. The PDF is set in the AAAI-27 author kit and names no track. The off-topic judgment applies to the tentative target. The file itself states no venue. NeSy-FM's CFP includes verifier-guided learning. That phrase is the entire contact. The manuscript's checker is an ordinary external program that scores an extracted integer (Section 1, p. 1). GRPO uses the bit as a reward. RFT uses it as an acceptance filter. The same section states the disclaimer that decides the fit question: "We do not claim a new architecture, a symbolic solver inside the model, or a general result about reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards." The paper proposes no neuro-symbolic architecture, no symbolic module inside the network, and no new coupling between a solver and a model. Verifier-guided learning on the CFP does not pull in a controlled GRPO-versus-RFT measurement on a procedural counting task. A NeSy-FM reviewer would have grounds to call the submission off-topic.
